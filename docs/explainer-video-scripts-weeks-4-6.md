# Week 4 — Influence & Impact
## Video: Influence Without Forcing — How Executives Create Buy-In
**Suggested duration:** 6–8 minutes

As leaders progress into more senior roles, something interesting happens.

They gain more authority — but successful leadership becomes less dependent on authority.

At executive level, many of the people you need to influence may not report to you. They may be fellow executives, board members, clients, regulators, partners, investors, senior specialists, or employees whose genuine commitment cannot simply be commanded.

This is why executive leadership requires influence.

Influence is the ability to shape thinking, behaviour or decisions without relying solely on positional power.

An executive who constantly needs to remind people of their title may technically have authority, but may have very little influence.

Strong executives create a different response. People listen because they trust their judgement. They understand their reasoning. They believe the leader understands the wider picture. And they feel that their concerns have been considered.

There are four important foundations of executive influence.

The first is credibility.

Before people evaluate your proposal, they often evaluate you. Do you understand the issue? Have you considered the consequences? Are you consistent? Do you follow through? Do you appear prepared?

Credibility is accumulated long before an important meeting begins.

The second foundation is relationships.

Leadership influence does not begin when you suddenly need something from someone. Senior leaders invest in relationships before moments of negotiation or disagreement occur. They understand their stakeholders, know what different departments are trying to achieve, listen carefully and build trust.

The third foundation is relevance.

One of the most common communication mistakes is presenting an idea entirely from your own perspective.

Executives learn to ask: Why should this matter to the person listening?

A finance executive may want to understand cost and financial exposure. An operations leader may care about implementation. A board member may be thinking about governance and organisational risk. Employees may be concerned about workload, certainty or how a decision affects their role.

The same recommendation may therefore need to be framed differently for different audiences.

That is not manipulation. It is intelligent communication — provided the facts, risks and intentions remain honest.

The fourth foundation is clarity.

Executive influence becomes stronger when people can quickly understand: What is happening? Why does it matter? What are you recommending? What are the consequences of doing nothing? And what decision is required?

Consider the difference between these two statements.

The first leader says: “We have been experiencing several challenges in the department and I think we really need to look at the way we are working.”

The second says: “Staff turnover has increased significantly over the past six months. If the trend continues, we are likely to experience operational delays and increased recruitment costs. I recommend that we conduct a 30-day diagnostic and present three corrective options to EXCO.”

The second message is easier to act upon.

Influence also requires understanding resistance.

When someone challenges your proposal, avoid immediately interpreting disagreement as disrespect. Instead ask: What concern is driving the resistance?

Sometimes people are protecting budgets. Sometimes they are protecting reputation. Sometimes they fear additional workload. Sometimes they simply do not understand the proposal.

A strong leader becomes curious before becoming defensive.

You can say: “Help me understand what concerns you most about this approach.”

Or: “What would need to be addressed for you to support this?”

That changes the conversation from confrontation to problem-solving.

There is also an important distinction between influence and manipulation.

Manipulation hides intention, distorts information or uses pressure primarily for personal advantage.

Ethical executive influence makes the objective clear, presents evidence honestly, respects the other person’s agency and seeks an outcome that can be defended organisationally.

As you develop as an executive, ask yourself: When I enter a room, what gives my words weight?

Is it only my position?

Or have I developed credibility, relationships, strategic understanding and the ability to connect ideas to what matters?

Your leadership impact is not measured only by how well you communicate.

It is measured by what becomes possible because you communicated.

That is the transition from executive presence to executive influence.

# Week 5 — Resilience & Self-Leadership
## Video: The Leader Under Pressure — Resilience, Emotional Regulation and Self-Leadership
**Suggested duration:** 6–8 minutes

You can learn a great deal about a leader when everything is going well.

But you often learn much more when things are not.

When targets are missed. When employees are frustrated. When the board is asking difficult questions. When an important client leaves. When people criticise your decision. Or when you simply do not have enough information to know what the perfect decision should be.

Executive leadership is performed under pressure.

The question is not whether pressure will occur.

The question is: Who do you become when it does?

This is where self-leadership becomes important.

Self-leadership is the ability to manage your own thoughts, emotions, behaviours and priorities so that you can continue acting in accordance with your responsibilities and values.

It begins with self-awareness.

Every leader has pressure patterns.

Some people become controlling. Some become impatient. Some stop listening. Some withdraw. Some procrastinate because they fear making the wrong decision. Others make decisions too quickly simply because uncertainty feels uncomfortable.

None of these reactions automatically make someone a poor leader.

But an executive must know their patterns.

Because what is unmanaged internally eventually becomes visible externally.

Imagine receiving an email strongly criticising a decision you made.

Your immediate emotional reaction might be anger. Your first thought could be: “They clearly do not understand what I am dealing with.”

That reaction is human.

But executive self-leadership creates a space between the emotion and the response.

Instead of responding immediately, you might ask: What exactly has triggered me? Is there useful information in the criticism? What response serves the organisation rather than simply relieving my frustration?

That space is where leadership happens.

One useful approach is: Pause. Name. Evaluate. Respond.

First, pause.

Do not allow urgency to automatically dictate behaviour.

Second, name what is happening.

“I am frustrated.”

“I am feeling defensive.”

“I am worried about the consequences.”

Naming an emotional reaction often reduces its ability to operate invisibly.

Third, evaluate.

Ask: What are the facts? What assumptions am I making? What outcome do I actually need?

Fourth, respond.

Choose behaviour based on the outcome required rather than the emotion experienced.

Resilience also requires recognising the difference between pressure and capacity.

Senior leaders sometimes confuse resilience with endlessly absorbing more work.

But chronic exhaustion eventually affects judgement. It reduces patience, narrows thinking, damages relationships and makes leaders reactive.

Sustainable executive performance therefore requires recovery.

That might include protecting thinking time, delegating appropriately, creating boundaries, prioritising sleep and physical wellbeing, seeking perspective from trusted advisors and recognising when everything genuinely cannot be treated as urgent.

Another important part of resilience is your relationship with failure.

Senior leadership means making decisions without perfect certainty.

Not every decision will succeed.

Resilient leaders do not ignore mistakes. But neither do they allow mistakes to become permanent statements about their capability.

They ask: What happened? What was within my control? What did I fail to anticipate? What have I learned? What must change next time?

And then they move forward.

This is the difference between reflection and rumination.

Reflection produces learning.

Rumination repeatedly replays the experience without producing useful action.

Your team also watches how you handle difficulty.

If every problem creates panic in the leader, anxiety travels through the organisation.

If every challenge produces blame, people learn to hide problems.

But if the leader demonstrates calm accountability, realistic optimism and constructive problem-solving, the organisation learns something different.

Resilience is contagious.

So is dysregulation.

The aim is not to become emotionless.

Strong leaders experience emotion.

They simply do not surrender leadership responsibility to every emotion they experience.

As you consider your executive future, ask: What version of me appears when pressure increases?

And is that the person I want leading the organisation?

Executive resilience begins when you can answer that question accurately — and then deliberately develop the person you want to become.

# Week 6 — Leading Sustainable Change
## Video: From Vision to Reality — How Executives Lead Sustainable Change
**Suggested duration:** 7–8 minutes

Executive leadership is often associated with vision.

But organisations rarely fail because nobody had an idea.

They struggle because good ideas are not translated into sustained behaviour.

A leader may announce: “We are going to improve customer experience.” “We need a stronger performance culture.” “We are becoming more innovative.” “We need employees to take greater accountability.”

But announcing an aspiration is not the same as leading change.

Sustainable change happens when people understand why change is necessary, what is expected to change, what their role is, how progress will be measured and whether leadership behaviour actually supports the message.

This is where executive leadership becomes operational.

The first responsibility of a change leader is to create a compelling case for change.

People need to understand: Why now? What happens if nothing changes? What opportunity are we trying to create?

Good change communication therefore begins with reality.

Perhaps customer complaints are increasing. Perhaps competitors are moving faster. Perhaps costs are becoming unsustainable. Perhaps employee engagement has deteriorated. Or perhaps the organisation has an opportunity to grow but its existing systems cannot support that growth.

The leader connects that reality to a clear future direction.

The second responsibility is clarity.

Vague change creates anxiety.

If leadership says, “We need greater accountability,” employees may interpret that in twenty different ways.

What specific behaviours need to change? What decisions will be made differently? What will leaders stop doing? What will employees start doing? How will success be recognised?

The clearer the behavioural expectation, the easier implementation becomes.

The third responsibility is stakeholder engagement.

People do not experience organisational change equally.

A new operating model may create opportunity for one department and uncertainty for another.

A digital transformation may increase efficiency while making employees worry about their jobs.

A new performance system may excite high performers while creating anxiety for employees who are unclear about expectations.

Executives must therefore understand the human impact of change.

Resistance does not always mean people are negative.

Resistance can communicate important information.

People may be saying: “I do not understand.” “I do not trust the process.” “I do not believe leadership will follow through.” “I am worried about what this means for me.” Or: “We tried this before and nothing changed.”

Strong change leaders listen to resistance without allowing resistance to completely determine the direction.

The fourth responsibility is leadership consistency.

Nothing damages change faster than leaders asking employees to behave differently while continuing their own old behaviour.

Imagine leadership announces: “We want more open communication.”

But employees who challenge decisions are punished.

The real culture will remain silent.

Or leaders announce: “We must control costs.”

But executives continue unnecessary expenditure.

People observe behaviour more closely than slogans.

The fifth responsibility is execution.

Every strategic priority should eventually answer five questions.

Who owns it?

What exactly will happen?

By when?

How will progress be measured?

What happens when progress stalls?

Without ownership and measurement, change becomes an intention rather than an organisational discipline.

Finally, sustainable change requires reinforcement.

Organisations naturally drift toward familiar behaviour.

Leaders therefore need to review progress, recognise new behaviour, correct behaviour that contradicts the direction, remove obstacles and continue communicating until the change becomes normal.

As you reach the end of this programme, consider how the six weeks connect.

Week 1 asked: Who are you as a leader?

Week 2 asked: How do others experience your presence?

Week 3 asked: Can you communicate clearly when the conversation is difficult?

Week 4 asked: Can you influence people and create commitment?

Week 5 asked: Can you lead yourself when pressure increases?

And now Week 6 asks: Can you turn leadership intention into sustainable organisational change?

That is executive readiness.

It does not mean having every answer.

It means becoming capable of providing direction while continuing to learn.

It means communicating clearly without pretending uncertainty does not exist.

It means accepting responsibility without believing you must personally control everything.

And it means creating an environment where other people can perform, contribute and grow.

The final question is therefore not: “Have I completed a leadership programme?”

It is: What will people experience differently because I completed it?

Your executive leadership journey does not finish here.

This is where deliberate leadership begins.
